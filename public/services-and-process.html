Homes are often built for young, fully mobile occupants, leaving small obstacles that become serious hazards over time. Knowing when to plan home modifications keeps your household safe and comfortable. Watch for these signals:

  • Stepping over high tub walls feels risky: If stepping into a traditional bathtub causes hesitation or loss of balance, converting to a walk-in or roll-in shower eliminates a daily trip hazard.
  • Narrow doorways catch walkers or wheelchairs: Standard interior doors frequently measure too narrow for comfortable clearance, especially when turning off hallways into bathrooms.
  • Uneven exterior steps or raised door thresholds: Entering through the front door or garage with steps and raised thresholds creates friction for anyone using a cane or rolling aid.
  • Grabbing towel bars for balance: Towel bars are not engineered to support body weight. If you or a loved one rely on fixtures for stability, reinforced grab bars are needed right away.
  • Tight bathroom footprints: Lack of clear floor space prevents a caregiver from helping or stops a mobility device from turning freely.

During the initial two to three weeks, your contractor takes precise field measurements, drafts layouts, confirms product selections, and orders long-lead materials. This phase ensures permits are submitted to local building departments before demolition starts.

Taking two to four days, the crew removes existing fixtures, drywall, flooring, and tub surrounds down to the studs. This stage exposes framing and subfloors, allowing the team to inspect for hidden moisture damage or framing issues that need repair.

Over three to five days, licensed trades relocate drain lines, adjust water supplies, vent exhaust fans outside, and run wiring for vanity lighting and GFCI outlets. Required rough inspections by local municipal inspectors occur right before any wall cavities are closed.

Spanning roughly one week, crews install moisture-resistant backer board, apply continuous shower waterproofing membranes, and lay your floor tile. Proper waterproofing requires adequate curing time to prevent future leaks behind finished surfaces.

Taking four to seven days, craftsmen lay wall tile, build out niches or benches, and apply grout and sealants. Custom tile designs require precision alignment and careful cutting around plumbing valves and corners.

In the final three to five days, plumbers set the vanity, sink, toilet, and shower trim, while electricians mount light fixtures and mirrors. A proper slope must be established toward the drain using a packed mortar bed or a pre-sloped manufactured tray. Done correctly, standing water can never pool under the finished tile layer.

Standard drywall must never be used. Install approved cementitious or waterproof backer boards securely, leaving proper expansion gaps and fastening according to manufacturer specifications.

Apply liquid or sheet waterproofing across all shower walls, the curb, and floor. Seal all corners, screw penetrations, niche transitions, and the drain flange with specialized waterproof bands and sealants to create a unified barrier.

Grout the open joints, clean away haze, and fill all changes of plane—such as inside corners and floor-to-wall transitions—with flexible sealant rather than rigid grout. Kitchen Bump-Out Addition vs Interior Layout Reconfiguration: What Fits Your Home?

Homeowners dealing with cramped cooking quarters often debate whether to extend their foundation or remodel within their current exterior walls. Both strategies deliver functional improvements, but they involve distinct structural work, timelines, and municipal approvals.

An interior layout reconfiguration removes non-bearing partitions, integrates dining nooks, or borrows unused space from adjacent closets or hallways. Because work stays inside the existing exterior envelope, you avoid foundation excavation, roofing tie-ins, and exterior wall modifications. Construction timelines are generally shorter, typically running three to six months for complete transformations, while focusing on open-concept conversions, new cabinetry, and updated utility pathways.

A bump-out kitchen addition, by contrast, physically extends your home's exterior boundary by several feet or adds an entirely new room extension. This approach provides significant square footage for walk-in pantries, extensive islands, and secondary prep zones that interior reconfiguration cannot accommodate. However, an expansion requires foundation footings, framing adjustments, exterior siding, roofing tie-ins, and detailed zoning setback compliance.

What Can Change the Right Choice

If your surrounding rooms are already sized efficiently and you cannot borrow interior space without hurting home functionality, an exterior addition is your best route. When structural changes alter your exterior envelope or foundation, working with a licensed general contractor is essential. Choosing between a floating vanity and a freestanding cabinet comes down to how you balance usable internal volume against the visual weight of the room. Both designs solve specific storage headaches, but they perform very differently depending on your bathroom's layout and structural framing.

How to Compare Your Options

A floating vanity mounts directly to the wall studs, leaving the floor underneath completely visible. This visual break makes compact bathrooms feel significantly more open, makes floor cleaning simple, and allows you to customize the counter height perfectly for your household. The tradeoff is reduced interior storage capacity because the lower half of the cabinet is eliminated, and you must install heavy-duty horizontal wall blocking during rough carpentry to support the vanity and stone countertop securely.

A freestanding vanity rests directly on the floor, offering maximum drawer depth and cupboard volume. It covers older flooring gaps, hides standard rough-in plumbing without requiring wall-pipe alterations, and generally involves a faster installation process. However, freestanding models can make a narrow bathroom feel crowded, and the tight seams between the cabinet sides and adjoining walls can create hard-to-clean dust traps.

Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Consider your plumbing setup first. If your drain and water lines come up through the floor rather than out of the wall, a freestanding vanity conceals them neatly, whereas a floating unit requires relocating plumbing into the wall cavity. Question: How do I know if my kitchen renovation needs municipal building permits, and what does the process involve?

Answer

A kitchen refresh that merely swaps existing cabinets in place or updates a light fixture without changing wiring generally does not trigger extensive plan reviews. However, the moment your complete kitchen renovation alters walls, relocates sink plumbing, moves a gas range, reconfigures ventilation ductwork, or adds dedicated electrical circuits for modern appliances, local building departments require construction drawings and trade permits.

In communities across Salt Lake City and the Salt Lake Valley, municipal inspectors must inspect plumbing rough-ins, structural modifications, and electrical capacity before walls are closed up with drywall. Attempting to bypass this step can complicate home resale, create fire or water hazards, and result in stop-work orders from city code enforcement.

What Can Change the Right Choice

The overall scope of your layout changes dictates the depth of permitting required. Projects staying strictly within the existing footprint without moving service lines occasionally qualify for streamlined trade reviews. In contrast, removing a central wall to build an open-concept kitchen requires structural calculations and detailed drafting to confirm load distribution.

A licensed general contractor prepares the drawings, pulls the necessary permits, and coordinates site visits with local inspectors at each construction milestone.
